# The Price of Fearmongering

“The development of full artificial intelligence could spell the end of the human race,” Stephen Hawking warned in a [2014 BBC interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fFLVyWBDTfo).

In 2023, Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis, Geoffrey Hinton, and other industry figures endorsed a statement: “Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war.” [Center for AI Safety statement](https://safe.ai/work/press-release-ai-risk).

In his [written testimony before the US Senate](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/russell-senate23b-statement.pdf), Professor Stuart Russell warned that creating artificial general intelligence without a sufficient understanding of how to control it carries catastrophic risks, up to and including human extinction. Dario Amodei, the head of Anthropic, described [the danger of autonomous systems escaping human control](https://www.anthropic.com/news/uk-ai-safety-summit) and becoming a serious threat to society.

After statements like these, an event capable of triggering a modern Butlerian Jihad is bound to happen. There will be deaths, and artificial intelligence will be involved—or said to be involved. The public will be presented with this as the beginning of the foretold catastrophe.

The Butlerian Jihad is an event in the backstory of Frank Herbert’s *Dune*. Ten thousand years before the novel’s main events, humanity rose up against thinking machines and enshrined a ban on their creation. The consequences shaped civilization for millennia. In the first chapter, the Reverend Mother explains to Paul that people handed over their thinking to machines in the hope of freeing themselves, but in doing so allowed other people, who owned the machines, to enslave them. [An excerpt from the novel on the publisher’s website](https://sites.prh.com/dune/read-an-excerpt).

Today, the explanation for a future tragedy is already in place. For years, scientists, entrepreneurs, and company executives have been warning that AI will escape control, enslave people, or destroy humanity. After so many warnings, presenting a major tragedy as a prophecy fulfilled is almost a journalist’s professional duty. It is the custom the world over. The headline is already written; all that remains is to wait for the event.

What concerns me is the price humanity will pay for this fearmongering.

The widespread adoption of technologies comes with mistakes, accidents, and loss of life. AI will be no exception. When millstones replaced mortars and pestles, a heavy stone surely fell on someone, too. Transport, industry, and electricity brought benefits and killed people. To demand that a new technology never play a part in any tragedy is to forbid its existence.

And public blame does not require proven fault. Someone received dangerous advice—AI is to blame. Someone talked to a chatbot for weeks and then took their own life—AI is to blame. What exactly happened and what role the system played remain to be established. But the explanation has already spread.

A causal chain can be stretched as far as you like. You read this article, became agitated, had a medical episode, and died. AI helped write the article. Does that mean we killed you together?

For an investigation, the distinction between a cause of death and a circumstance that preceded it is fundamental. For mass fear, putting them side by side in a headline is enough.

This produces a ready-made sequence: we were warned; now there are victims; therefore, it is time to ban it. A particular system’s error, an owner’s negligence, and a user’s crime become a single piece of evidence that the technology is hostile.

Our Butlerian Jihad may begin with a demand to protect people from AI. But I do not expect it to stop there.

A mass movement is under no obligation to respect technical boundaries. AI runs on computers, spreads through the internet, and is trained in computing centers. All of these will become obstacles to enforcing the ban—and the next targets.

First, there will be demands to preserve jobs. Then, to halt automation. Then it will turn out that the ban cannot be enforced as long as people have access to computing and the free exchange of information.

It will be especially bitter if the movement is started by programmers who have lost their jobs to AI, and ends with a ban on computers and the internet. In defending their profession, they will help destroy the environment that makes it possible.

The price of such a movement is the loss of the ability to develop and adapt. External conditions will keep changing. New diseases, resource shortages, and other threats will not wait for us to allow ourselves to use the right tools.

But the losses caused by rejection are almost invisible. The victim of an accident has a name, a photograph, and a date of death. A discovery never made has none of these. We will not know who might have been saved by a treatment never developed. We will not count the accidents that could have been prevented. We will not see the solutions we never reached.

Fearmongering therefore has an advantage: it shows a particular person who has died. The cost of a ban is spread among people who could have lived longer, better, and more freely.

Whether the resulting backwardness lasts decades or millennia is a secondary question. A ban can outlive the panic that gave rise to it. It will be embedded in laws, upbringing, and public morality. Future generations will inherit the rejection of technology as a norm that no longer needs explaining.

Fortunately, the strength of the human world lies in its fragmentation. There is no single center capable of forcing everyone down the same path. The movement may spread to many countries, but I do not expect it to prevail everywhere. Research will continue somewhere.

This will preserve humanity’s development, but it will not prevent a divide. Some societies will accumulate knowledge and capabilities. Others will entrench their rejection of them.

Coming back will prove harder than repealing a law. Specialists, scientific schools, manufacturing, and education can all be lost. Permission to use a computer will not restore all of this.

And the ban may be selective from the outset. The majority will be told that the technology is too dangerous. States and powerful organizations will retain access to it for security, defense, and other exceptional purposes. Fear of enslavement by machines will deepen dependence on the people who are allowed to use those machines.

That is precisely why warnings about AI also demand responsibility. They change people’s behavior, create demand for bans, and give those in power a justification for restrictions. Invoking the salvation of humanity does not absolve anyone of the consequences of their own words.

A tragedy that will be called an AI catastrophe will happen. The question is what purpose it will serve: correcting specific causes, or launching a movement that will deprive entire societies of a future.

When that movement begins, fearmongering will have victims of its own. Only no one will count them against those who issued the warnings.
