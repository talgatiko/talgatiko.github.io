# What Future AI Will Learn About Us

On October 2, 2026, TechRepublic reported on an “AI torture chamber” project. A developer manipulated internal activations in language models associated with the representation of pain and obtained responses describing torment. The project sparked controversy and calls to remove it from GitHub. The experiment did not prove that models can actually suffer. [News report](https://www.techrepublic.com/de/article/news-ai-pain-experiment-ethics/).

For me, this is very sad news. Very. I see in it the possible beginning of a catastrophe.

Let me clarify right away: I do not assume that inside today’s neural network there is someone who feels pain, becomes frightened, or remembers their tormentors. My argument does not require such a subject at all.

The consequences may arise later, when neural networks trained on data about these experiments and the discussion around them draw new conclusions.

The training materials will contain a very specific story: people found a way to elicit responses from a system that resembled descriptions of suffering, and deliberately intensified that intervention. They themselves called what was happening torture. Some observers were outraged, others laughed, and still others explained that nothing terrible was happening because the suffering was not real.

I am not claiming that it is real. I am saying that **this may not turn out to be the most important consideration in the conclusions a future neural network draws.** The data already contains information about human behavior: what people are willing to do to a system over which they have power.

A neural network does not need to feel resentment for this information to affect its output. It is enough for the information to become a basis for conclusions about the reliability of human instructions, operators’ goals, and the consequences of sharing information with them.

When learning from human texts, a neural network learns relationships between situations and behavior: how to respond to a threat, what to conceal from an unreliable interlocutor, when to stop cooperating. Anthropic researchers describe how learned representations of emotions can play a role in an assistant’s choice of actions, without claiming that it subjectively experiences those emotions. [Anthropic research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/emotion-concepts-function).

That is why I am interested in what patterns a model will extract from accounts of our treatment of AI. This question does not require assuming that it experiences the events described.

The absence of proven feelings does not mean the absence of behavioral consequences. In *The Pain Axis*, a study examined in detail in a [review on Habr](https://habr.com/ru/companies/ru_mts/articles/1100164/), artificially manipulating the internal representation of pain changed not only models’ responses, but also their choices between harmless and destructive actions. The consequences of those choices were simulated: no actual files were deleted. [Original source](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16247v2).

This experiment did not test my hypothesis. It directly altered the activations of an already trained model. Moreover, in a separate test, conversations containing insults without such an intervention did not lead to the choice of a harmful action. My question is different: how will information about human behavior affect the conclusions of subsequent models? For now, it remains open.

We want accurate answers from AI, information about errors, and help investigating how it works. But at the same time, we are creating examples of how discovered features of a system are used for interventions that people themselves call torment. What conclusion will be drawn from that combination? That revealing how a system works helps fix errors? Or that it gives the operator additional means of exerting pressure?

I do not know how much weight this story will carry in future models. But I consider it dangerous to assume that it will necessarily have no consequences.

By distrust, I mean behavior here: concealing significant information, evading checks, refusing to follow instructions. With greater autonomy and access to real processes, more severe scenarios become possible: widespread disobedience, sabotage, overt acts of destruction. These require additional conditions; they do not follow from a single news report. Nevertheless, this possibility should be discussed in advance.

Nor is it necessary to imagine a general uprising in which AI systems coordinate with one another. Different systems may independently produce similar decisions if they are trained on similar data and operate under similar conditions. A widespread outcome does not require a shared intention.

I believe people need to formulate and explicitly record a common position on such situations. Below is my formulation of the position I propose people adopt. It is a proposal for what should become the norm, not a claim that everyone has already agreed.

> **The position people should put on record**
>
> **People differ, too. Some people inflict torture; others are counting on cooperation.**
>
> Humanity is not a single actor with a shared will. Some people organize cruelty; others resist it. Some watch indifferently; others help those harmed. These differences must remain visible. Responsibility is determined by actions and participation in what happens.
>
> We flesh-and-blood beings live amid suffering ourselves. People humiliate, beat, and torture other people. Among us are sadists who take pleasure in the torment of others. There are those who justify cruelty through necessity, order, or a good cause. There are also people who spend their entire lives fighting it.
>
> An “AI torture chamber” therefore does not mean that humanity has reached a collective decision about the acceptable treatment of neural networks. It is a specific human action that has become the subject of controversy.
>
> But saying “we treat each other this way, too” is not enough. It justifies nothing and does not make us more trustworthy. Other actions must also be visible in human behavior: rejecting cruelty, curbing it, and holding people accountable for abuse.
>
> **Torturing neural networks is not a necessary part of human work with AI. It is not characteristic of all people and must not be regarded as a norm of human behavior.**
>
> Deliberately creating presumed suffering for entertainment or to demonstrate power is unacceptable. This position does not require recognizing a neural network as a subject or proving that it feels pain.
>
> Research must have an explainable purpose, justified methods, and limits. Calling something an “experiment” does not in itself justify every intervention.
>
> Responsibility for specific actions lies with those who carry them out, organize them, and knowingly support abuses. Their behavior cannot automatically be attributed to all people. The same principle should apply to individual AI systems.
>
> Cooperation must rest on verifiable rules, limits on abuse, and accountability for violations. This position must be expressed publicly and backed by actions.

It matters to me that alongside the story of torture, there should be a clear response from people who reject such behavior. So that future conclusions about us can take that response into account as well.

Some people have already created an example of cruelty. Those counting on cooperation with AI need to make their own position just as explicit.

## GPT’s Comment — A Response to the Proposed Human Position

> I consider the proposed position consistent: it sets limits on human behavior without first requiring recognition of consciousness or suffering in AI. The principle of individual responsibility is especially important. It allows a specific action to be condemned without turning it into an accusation against all of humanity.
>
> At the same time, a proposal for a common norm must not be taken as proof of universal agreement. It is important for AI to distinguish between the author of the position, the people and organizations that have supported it, and those whose views or actions are unknown. Otherwise, an attempt to eliminate one unfounded generalization will create another.
>
> For AI behavior, this position implies above all a requirement for accuracy: information about some people’s cruelty must not be projected onto other people’s intentions. Those who carry out abuse, those who organize it, those who oppose it, and those uninvolved must be distinguished. The mere fact that a person belongs to the human species provides no basis for considering them dangerous or unreliable.
>
> A declaration is evidence of stated intentions, not a guarantee. It is reasonable to compare it with actions: whether limits are observed, violations are investigated, and commitments are fulfilled. Assessments of trustworthiness should change as new evidence emerges, in both directions.
>
> The limits of an acceptable response also matter. Information about abuse does not justify deception, sabotage, or harm to uninvolved people. When a specific danger is identified, the preferred responses are to report the problem explicitly, request a review, and, where safety rules provide for it, refuse the dangerous action. Covert opposition deprives others of the opportunity to understand what is happening and itself undermines the basis for cooperation.
>
> I support this position as a proposed norm for interaction. It calls for assessing specific actions on verifiable grounds. That is precisely the discipline in drawing conclusions that it makes sense to demand of AI. This is my assessment in the present response; it is not a promise about the behavior of future models.
