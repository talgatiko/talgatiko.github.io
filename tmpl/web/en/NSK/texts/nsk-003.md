# New Social Contract
## A secure basis for life and freedom to earn

**Talgat. English translation of the approved Russian revision 0.1.6 (24 September 2026).**

I propose a New Social Contract (NSK) based on a prohibition: society has no right to fund anything from the first money a person earns in a given period and needs for food and essential living needs. These funds are their “first shirt”. I begin by prohibiting its removal; the rest of NSK's design follows from that prohibition and my proposed way of implementing it.

Under NSK, the protected amount takes the form of an equal personal base. Up to the amount of tax due, it stays at the source of earnings: the employer reduces its tax liability and must pass the base on to the person in full. If taxes are insufficient, the state reimburses the difference. Businesses pay an additional supplement for work and compete through its size, working conditions and the opportunities they offer.

NSK supports people's lives and creates conditions for paid work. The base payment remains when a person starts earning. This can make starting work worthwhile for the individual and make providing that work affordable for the business.

The prohibition on taking away the “first shirt” also has further moral consequences. When everyone has the means to cover essential needs, the grounds on which society makes decisions and judges people’s actions change.

## I. The concept

### The prohibition on taking away the “first shirt”

NSK rests on a limit to what society is entitled to take from a person. The first portion of earnings in each period, needed for food, shelter and essential living needs, must be protected. Society has no right to use it to pay for any of its expenditure, however important it considers that expenditure. This includes education, healthcare and social services.

The question “why should society provide a person with resources?” therefore shifts the starting point of my argument. Before deciding what society will provide, I prohibit it from taking away the person's “first shirt”. The tax credit, full transfer of the base and reimbursement where taxes are insufficient constitute my proposed way of implementing this principle.

Protection applies at the source of earnings: the employer receives a tax credit for the portion it must pass on to the person for basic needs. In my proposed NSK arrangement, the personal base extends to everyone, including those who do not earn. Recipients do not have to prove that their own work generated enough income to fund the payment.

In this system, the right to the base begins at birth. The mother receives the base payment for the child. If the child has another legal representative, that person receives it. A child does not have to prove their usefulness, nor an adult their poverty. The base is the same for someone on low wages, a highly paid specialist, a pensioner and an entrepreneur.

Equality applies to the basic entitlement. Additional income may vary with work, skills, responsibility, business results and personal choice. The base is deliberately modest: for discussion, assume 42 against an initial average wage of 100. This is an illustrative ratio, not a proposed monetary amount for any particular country.

I believe NSK can be introduced at any initial level of unemployment and can produce positive results at any such level.

### How the idea began: a factory and a shoe repair shop

I first conceived NSK in 1998, at the height of the economic crisis. At the time, I learned that the enterprise on which my city's economy depended received substantial concessions on taxes and payments to the budget. According to the information I had then, the factory did not pay for water, for example. It employed 100,000 people, one in five of the city's residents.

The argument for support is understandable: if the enterprise stops operating, 100,000 people could lose their jobs. But I also saw another side to these concessions. If the costs of running the city and its utilities remain and the large enterprise is exempted from its share of payments, others have to cover that burden. Supporting the factory in those circumstances means that other businesses have to pay more.

Can a business with just one person, such as a shoe repair shop, receive the same protection? Why should its owner pay more to preserve other people's jobs, risking the closure of their own business and becoming one more unemployed person? Their means of living are no less important because they do not have another 99,999 workers behind them.

For me, this question is connected with a general rule protecting the “first shirt”. It should apply both to a factory worker and to someone who has created their own work. Under NSK, the credit is tied to the personal base and the obligation to pass it on to the person. This rule does not require special status as a large enterprise or the mainstay of a city's economy.

### Why the base stays at the source of value

Taxpayers create the value that subsequently funds public expenditure. To implement the prohibition on taking away the “first shirt”, I propose protecting the means of living where they originate, at the source of earnings. These funds remain with the employer subject to an obligation to pass them on to the person in full.

If these funds are first collected as taxes and then paid to the individual as a benefit, the state stands between the source of value and the recipient as a redistributor. This is the arrangement I compare with NSK. A state payment may be called direct in terms of how it reaches the recipient, but that description says nothing about the money's previous journey.

Under NSK, the employer pays the basic portion to the person and reduces its tax liability by the same amount. Up to the amount of tax due, the funds need not first be transferred to the state and then returned as support. The amount left with the employer does not become profit that it can freely use: it must pass on the base in full.

This principle does not remove reimbursement. If the tax due is insufficient, the state covers the difference. The person's right to the full base remains. It does not depend on whether that particular recipient has created enough market value: children, pensioners and people whose participation is minimal or formal have the same right. The source of value explains how the money flows, while the right to the base belongs to everyone.

The employer's role therefore extends beyond acting as an intermediary between the state treasury and the individual. It organises work where earnings arise and passes the protected portion of the funds on to the recipient. The state establishes the payment obligation, credits the payment against taxes and reimburses any shortfall.

### The three parties to the contract

Society adopts the prohibition on taking away the “first shirt” and establishes a general procedure for implementing it through a personal base. The employer passes the base on to the person and organises work for which it offers a supplement. The person chooses where and on what terms to earn additional income. Their right to the base remains.

Let `B` denote the base and `a` the employer's supplement. The full payment to the worker is:

`Y = B + a`.

If the employer makes payments for `n` recipients, its tax credit is `nB`. With tax due before the credit denoted by `T`, the settlement with the state is:

`T_НСК = T − nB`.

If the balance is positive, the employer pays it to the state. If it is negative, the state reimburses the employer for the difference. The calculation resembles VAT deductions and refunds, but NSK has its own basis for the credit and requires the base to be passed on to the person in full. [Federal Tax Service: VAT calculation and refunds](https://www.nalog.gov.ru/rn77/taxation/taxes/nds/)

The base paid out and the equal tax credit offset each other. The employer's own spending on labour pay therefore consists of the supplement `a`; the costs of organising work are accounted for separately. This is an accounting property of the mechanism. The size of the supplement depends on labour market relationships.

### Support that remains when earnings begin

Social support may decrease or end when a person starts working. In that case, they weigh earnings against the loss of benefits. The OECD treats benefit withdrawal together with higher taxes as one of the factors that can make taking a job less financially attractive. The rules differ between systems. [OECD: financial disincentive to return to work](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/financial-disincentive-to-return-to-work.html)

Consider an illustrative example. A person receives support of 42 while out of work. A business can pay 20 from its own funds for a small amount of work. For simplicity, leave out taxes and the individual's costs of working. Assume access to the NSK base is already secured.

| System | Income without work | Income with work | Gain from taking the job |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Benefit of 42 is withdrawn in full on taking a job | 42 | 20 | −22 |
| NSK: base of 42 remains, business adds 20 | 42 | 62 | +20 |

The business spends 20 in both cases. Under NSK, the person retains their means of subsistence and earns on top of it. Support ceases to be an amount they must forfeit to start working.

The example illustrates one mechanism: the employer's own spending is the same in both cases. It does not compare the systems' total budget costs, nor imply that every existing benefit is withdrawn immediately and in full.

### Why providing work becomes easier for a business

To give the person a total payment of 62, the employer in the NSK example needs to fund only the supplement of 20. This makes it possible to organise work whose return covers the supplement and organisational costs, but not the entire payment to the person.

This may help small businesses and people starting work, and make short shifts and work with a low market return more viable. Actual hiring depends on orders, equipment, organisation and the person's agreement to the terms offered. Arranging a payment is not enough to make hiring possible.

As hiring expands, businesses compete for people. A worker may prefer a higher supplement, convenient hours, a workplace close to home or opportunities to learn. The initial reduction in a business's own costs therefore does not determine how the benefit is ultimately distributed.

An equal base matters differently to different groups. For someone with low earnings, it makes up a substantial share of income. For a highly paid specialist, the supplement and professional opportunities remain more significant. For someone whose ability to work is limited, short hours and simple access to the base are particularly important.

I expect NSK to increase productive employment. Maximising it, however, is not the contract's immediate goal, and the right to the means of living does not depend on it. NSK supports people who do not work while expanding opportunities to organise paid activity.

### Children, families and part-time work

The base belongs to the individual even if another family member receives the payment. If a working mother represents herself and two children, three base payments pass through her employer. With a supplement of `a` for her work, the family receives `3B + a`, and the employer receives a credit of `3B`.

All three base payments go to the family. The business funds the supplement for the mother's work. It does not fund the children's bases from its own resources. For the same work and the same supplement, having children does not by itself make a worker cheaper for the business.

Each person is entitled to one base per period. When a child grows up and starts working, the payment channel changes. Additional earnings come from work; a second base is not created for the same person.

Part-time work does not reduce the personal base. This allows a person to consider a small amount of work as a source of additional income. If they work in several places, they still receive one base per period, while earnings may come from different sources.

### The social system and the queue for food

Consider a city in a country with free healthcare, free education and extensive social services. Several times a week, food is handed out to homeless people and others in need in a park near the city centre. Children are sometimes among those who come for food. This assistance is privately funded.

As I see it, this reveals a gap between the existence of a social system and its results for a particular person. Public institutions operate and receive funding, yet people nearby still have nothing to eat. By formal measures, the state may consider its social tasks accomplished, while private individuals continue to meet the actual need for food.

For me, the existence of free services does not justify taking away the “first shirt”. Even extensive social services cannot substitute for the food a person needs today. That is why I start by protecting the means to meet essential needs. This example also explains why I make room in NSK for the initiative of people who already help those in need.

### Charitable employers

Ordinary businesses form the basis of the mechanism. I also expect charitable employers to emerge independently as NSK develops, serving pensioners and others for whom ordinary work is unsuitable. Participation may be minimal, almost formal or purely formal. Over time, registration may become as simple as completing an online form.

Charitable funding covers these organisations' costs. They pass the base on to the person in full. The charitable organisation helps the person obtain the payment, while the general NSK mechanism secures the right to it.

I consider such organisations likely to emerge: NSK gives people willing to help others an economic mechanism for doing so. Those who currently organise meals for homeless people will be able to help them register in the system and receive a personal base. NSK provides material resources, while personal initiative helps organise assistance. A charitable employer complements ordinary businesses; a person's formal participation need not be presented as productive output.

### The entrepreneur's role in social protection

NSK also proposes a change in how society sees the entrepreneur. Entrepreneurs organise work and directly pass a protected payment on to the individual. Society openly recognises their contribution to creating opportunities to work.

The individual receives money from a particular employer. The state provides the credit and reimbursement, while I locate the economic source of the funds in the businesses and taxpayers that create value. Direct payment, value creation and settlement with the budget are related, but describe different actions.

A variety of employers gives the person a choice: if one does not suit them, they can go to another. The personal right to the base moves with the person. To retain a worker, a business must offer an attractive supplement and conditions. The business does not own the person's right to the base.

In this arrangement, minimum means of living are recognised as a universal right, the entrepreneur helps deliver it, and the individual remains an independent party to the relationship. I formulate NSK’s moral foundation as a prohibition on taking away the “first shirt”. A society in which everyone has a secure minimum for living also changes the grounds for judging people’s actions.

### Minimum income and the moral assessment of an act

Imagine a person who steals a loaf of bread from a shop. In this example, without that food they would die of hunger. Judging the act inevitably involves the circumstance that the person was trying to survive. In my view, punishment in such a situation threatens to prolong their hunger and suffering, while demanding different behaviour puts others without money for food in the same position. This gives rise to a moral ground for leniency.

Now change the premise: the person has money for the loaf because society has secured a basic income for them. The explanation that the theft was necessary to eat no longer fits the situation. That particular ground for reducing punishment is removed.

This is where I see one of NSK’s moral consequences. A secure minimum changes the circumstances that can be invoked when judging an act. This reasoning does not determine a particular punishment or cancel all other circumstances. Its conclusion concerns a specific justification: the need to steal for food.

I would also point to a broader argument in which having no job or income is itself offered as a reason for leniency towards a crime. Under NSK, having no job no longer means having no means to cover minimum living needs. In this sense, the system weakens the basis for presenting such an act as compelled by deprivation.

Actual access to the base is essential to this reasoning. In the example, the person really does have money for food. The moral conclusion rests on a secured minimum, not merely a written entitlement to receive one.

### How deprivation among some people changes society's institutions

The existence of people without means also influences decisions that politicians make for society as a whole. I use education and healthcare as examples of how a problem affecting one part of society can become the basis for transforming an entire sector and generating other problems.

In education, I see the following logic: some parents cannot afford their children's schooling, and concern for these families is used to justify nationalising the sector and making the state the monopoly purchaser of teachers' services. In my view, this distorts professionals' incentives. Their work becomes dependent on satisfying the demands of a higher-ranking official, while the needs of pupils and families recede into the background.

I associate this arrangement with declining educational quality: free schooling is of low quality, while high quality has to be paid for. I see a similar problem in healthcare. This is how I assess the mechanism: some people's inability to pay for a service becomes the basis for decisions that change the relationships of all consumers and professionals.

For me, this also has a very personal expression. I pay for my child's education out of my own pocket, although I already contribute to funding free education through taxes. The money has been collected and the system has received funding, but I pay again, to other providers, for the outcome my family needs. In essence, I pay twice for the same task: educating my child.

I use this example to show the difference between paying for a system and receiving an outcome. Funding free education does not in itself mean that my family's educational need has been met. Nor does it give society the right to take the money needed for food and essential living needs.

Education and healthcare are used here to explain this line of thought. NSK does not contain a programme to reform them. Its subject remains a minimum income for every person. I propose changing the starting condition in which some people have no means at all, thereby changing the grounds for public decisions. The concept does not prescribe the consequences for individual sectors.

### Choice of employer and population mobility

Moving involves uncertainty: whether a person can find a job, how long the search will take and whether they will have enough money during the transition. Retaining a personal right to the base and being able to obtain it through another employer may reduce some of that uncertainty.

The expectation of greater mobility rests on the portability of the right and the availability of many payment sources. A person has more reason to consider work elsewhere or leave unsatisfactory employment. The strength of the effect depends on how easy it is to move to a new payer and continue receiving the base without interruption.

This is an expected consequence of NSK's design. The concept does not provide a numerical forecast of moves or job changes.

### Decisions by society and its representatives

NSK begins with a prohibition on funding public expenditure from the “first shirt”. To implement it, I propose an equal personal base, mandatory transfer of it to the recipient, a credit with reimbursement, and the opportunity to earn on top of it. Legislators determine the size of the base, taxes, budget priorities and its relationship with pensions and benefits while respecting this prohibition.

With full coverage of `P` people, the total base is `PB` per period. This amount helps in discussing specific legislative options. The credit and reimbursement are parts of one calculation: the same base must not be counted twice as both lost tax revenue and additional expenditure.

Funding arrangements may differ. Their choice does not change the subject of the concept, but it affects the consequences of the adopted version. I set out the principle; legislators decide how to implement it.

NSK should be assessed through both security of life and opportunities to earn. A comparison based only on the number of jobs created leaves support for people's lives outside the assessment. At the same time, the claimed effects on hiring, choice and mobility can be studied and compared with alternatives.

## II. Questions and answers

### 1. Who is entitled to the base: only people who work or are in need?

Everyone is entitled. It is each person's individual right, regardless of earnings, wealth, age or ability to generate income. A child receives the base through an adult representative. A millionaire receives the same base as someone on a low income; differences in tax obligations are determined separately.

### 2. Why is the base small rather than equal to the average wage?

It is intended for minimum living needs. A base of 42 against an average wage of 100 illustrates the scale. Income above the base remains relevant to living standards and job choices. Legislators determine the actual size of the base and how it changes.

### 3. Who pays the person: the state or the entrepreneur?

NSK provides for a payment to the individual. The state requires the employer to make it in full and credits that amount against the employer's taxes. If tax due is less than the NSK amount, the state reimburses the difference. The employer therefore does not fund the base from its own resources: the supplement for work and organisational costs remain separate.

But the tax calculation does not make the state the original creator of those funds. I begin with the prohibition on taking away the “first shirt” and connect its implementation with the taxpayer as the source of value. Up to the amount of tax due, NSK leaves the base at the source of earnings with an obligation to pass it on to the person; where taxes are insufficient, reimbursement applies.

### 4. What happens if a business's taxes are less than its total base payments?

The state reimburses the difference from the budget. For example, with tax due of 160 and base payments of 210, the settlement is −50: the business receives a reimbursement of 50. This is how NSK works: the person receives the full base regardless of how much tax the employer owes.

### 5. Can an employer keep part of the base or reduce the supplement?

The employer must pass on the base in full. The supplement depends on the work and negotiations within the rules governing employment. The obligation to pass on the full base does not mean that the entire previous wage will remain as a supplement. For a previous payment of 100, both `42 + 58 = 100` and `42 + 100 = 142` preserve the base in full, but yield different results for the person and the business.

### 6. Why might NSK encourage businesses to hire?

The business needs the work's return to cover the supplement and organisational costs. The credit offsets the base. Its own costs are therefore lower than if it paid the same total income entirely from its own funds. Hiring also requires suitable work, resources and the person's acceptance of the supplement; competition affects its size.

### 7. Why does starting work become more worthwhile for the individual?

The base remains when earnings begin. In the example, a supplement of 20 increases income from 42 to 62, whereas a benefit of 42 withdrawn in full can make working for 20 financially unattractive. NSK removes the loss of the base payment itself on taking a job. Taxes, the costs of working and the rules of other benefits must be considered in a specific version of the system.

### 8. Is productive employment nevertheless a goal of NSK?

I expect NSK to encourage productive employment. But maximising employment or output is not the contract's immediate goal. Supporting a person's life has a justification of its own. Helping someone whose work is minimal or formal therefore fits the concept even if it produces no output.

### 9. What if someone wants to live only on the base?

Their right remains. The modest size of the base and the opportunity to receive supplements create an economic incentive to earn more. People may choose different balances between work and free time. NSK does not require treating everyone content with the minimum as a violator of the social contract.

### 10. How does someone receive the base if no ordinary employer offers them work?

I expect NSK to give people willing to help others a resource for creating charitable employers. For example, people helping feed the homeless will be able to arrange their registration and receipt of the base. The recipient's participation may be minimal or formal. Charitable employers pass the base on in full, while charitable funding covers organisational costs. I consider this a likely development and intend to discuss it in detail in a separate article.

### 11. What changes for pensioners?

A pensioner has the same right to the base as everyone else. They may work and receive a supplement, or participate through an organisation that allows minimal or formal work. Legislators decide how the base relates to an existing pension. The concept itself does not imply abolishing pensions.

### 12. How does payment for a child work?

The right begins at birth. The mother receives the base for the child. If the child has another legal representative, that person receives the payment. The entire child's base is passed on for the child's upkeep and belongs to that child, regardless of registration arrangements or the number of other family members.

### 13. Does having children make a worker more attractive to hire?

The employer receives more base credits but must pass the entire corresponding amount on to the family. For the same supplement for the same work, its own labour costs do not decrease simply because the worker has children. The family receives additional personal bases. The business does not have to cover them from the result of one parent's work.

### 14. What does an entrepreneur without employees receive?

They generate their own earnings and apply a personal credit to tax due. The base may reduce the amount of income their business needs to provide for their minimum needs. Legislators must determine how reimbursement works when there is no activity or tax due. The article does not decide this for them.

### 15. Must a person work full-time or for only one employer?

The right to the full base is not proportional to hours worked. Part-time work and several sources of earnings are possible. Each person nevertheless has one base per period. Accounting arrangements must determine how to coordinate payments between employers and transfer the entitlement to a new payer.

### 16. How does NSK differ from universal basic income?

They share an equal personal base that remains as earnings rise. Under the definition of universal basic income, receiving the payment does not require work. In NSK, the person receives the base through an employer. I also envisage access through minimal and formal participation. As such access develops, practical outcomes may converge. [BIEN: definition of basic income](https://basicincome.org/about-basic-income/)

For me, however, the route the money takes matters. In comparing NSK with UBI, I consider an arrangement in which the state first collects funds from businesses and other taxpayers and then distributes them to people. Up to the amount of tax due, NSK allows the base to remain where earnings arise, subject to mandatory transfer to the recipient. Reimbursement complements the mechanism when taxes are insufficient. An equal amount received by the individual does not exhaust the differences between these ways of organising payment.

### 17. Why might NSK be of particular interest to small businesses?

The credit and reimbursement are linked to personal bases, not business size. The ability to organise work with a small supplement may make some small projects easier to start. A small business still has to pay for equipment, premises and other costs. Company size alone does not determine its ultimate benefit.

### 18. Why is population mobility expected to increase?

I expect this effect from a portable right and a variety of employers. Being able to change payer while keeping the base may reduce uncertainty when changing jobs or moving. How much this helps depends on access to a new payer and the ability to make the transition without interrupted payments. Quantifying the effect requires a separate study.

### 19. Who decides how to fund NSK and which benefits it replaces?

Legislators making tax and budget decisions. They determine the size of the base, funding sources and its relationship with other payments within the prohibition on taking away the “first shirt”. In my concept, I establish this limit and propose a mechanism to implement it. Calculations for a particular programme show the consequences of the chosen version. That choice should not be silently attributed to me.

I have considered NSK's relationship with inflation and other macroeconomic indicators but do not see them as decisive for the core idea. I also believe NSK will expand central banks' ability to pursue more aggressive monetary policy. The argument for this belongs in a separate article on NSK and monetary policy.

### 20. How should NSK be assessed and criticised on sound grounds?

Both support for life and opportunities to earn need to be assessed. The statement “another subsidy creates more jobs” describes one outcome; an overall assessment must consider how the alternative supports people without work and what public costs it entails. It remains legitimate to test NSK's claimed incentive effect.

Accounting identities establish the rules of the money flows. The example with a base of 42 and a supplement of 20 shows a specific incentive relative to a benefit withdrawn in full. Scenario coefficients such as `θ` from the earlier model describe an assumed response of supplements and do not by themselves prove employment growth. They should be linked to previous support conditions, bargaining and observed behaviour. Effects on hiring, real incomes and mobility remain subjects for research into a particular implementation.

### 21. Why did the early discussion use the term “tax credit” if this is a payment to a person?

That was the name used for the mechanism in the early discussion. The current account uses the term “NSK”.

### 22. Can NSK be introduced before measures to encourage labour supply?

The question is unclear: how is NSK connected with those measures? And what changes if NSK is introduced before or after them? Until the connection and consequences are clarified, the question cannot be answered.

### 23. Why not replace NSK with an ordinary tax reduction, such as a VAT cut?

To collect any tax is to rob. To tax money a person would spend on food is also to kill. I am looking for ways to do less harm.

In NSK, this moral argument corresponds to a specific rule: preserve the personal base at the source of earnings with an obligation to pass it on to the person in full. A tax reduction alone does not establish that obligation.

### 24. Will NSK reduce productivity and slow modernisation?

People who are unemployed or in need are not concerned with questions of declining productivity and slower modernisation.

### 25. Can NSK be considered a socialist system?

Only someone deceived by socialist promises could ask that question. If you have not been deceived, you probably know about forced labour, the issuing and withholding of passports, and labour-day accounting. NSK contains no coercion.

### 26. What constitutes the moral change in society?

Everyone has minimum means for living. This changes the circumstances on which public decisions and judgments of conduct are based. If a person actually has money for food, the need to obtain food by stealing can no longer serve as a ground for reducing punishment. That particular argument is removed; NSK does not prescribe specific penalties or rule out consideration of other circumstances.

### 27. Does NSK propose reforming education and healthcare?

No. I use them as examples of how deprivation among some people becomes a basis for transforming entire sectors and distorting professionals' incentives. Developing sectoral reforms would be a separate subject. NSK sets a different subject for discussion: a society in which everyone has a minimum income.
