# New Social Contract: in brief

People need money for food, housing and basic needs before their first wage arrives. The New Social Contract (NSK) proposes recognising this material basis as a personal right belonging to everyone from birth, regardless of income, age or labour productivity.

When a person starts working, the base remains. The person receives an additional supplement for work: `Y = B + a`, where `B` is the NSK base and `a` is the supplement. The person keeps the means to live and can choose where and on what terms to earn more without risking the base for a small supplement.

Under the proposed mechanism, the employer pays the NSK base. The state requires the employer to transfer the full amount to the person and credits it against the employer's taxes. If accrued taxes are insufficient, the state reimburses the difference. The employer pays the work supplement from its own funds and bears separate organisational costs; the base is transferred with a tax credit or reimbursement.

For illustration, let the base be 42 and the supplement be 20, giving the person 62. These figures show only payment arithmetic. They are not a ruble amount and do not forecast future wages. A person working part time still receives the full base. Multiple jobs do not create multiple bases for one period. A child's base belongs to the child and is paid through a representative.

For people who cannot access ordinary work, the author expects charitable payers to develop, with minimal or formal participation by the person. Charity would cover administration, while NSK would provide money to the person. This remains an author hypothesis about how the mechanism might develop; it does not mean such a system already exists.

NSK combines support for life with the possibility of additional earnings. The author expects that retaining the base while working may make employment, choosing an employer and mobility easier. The scale of these effects has not been established. The base amount, financing and interaction with pensions and benefits must be determined in a specific legislative programme. Budget neutrality and the absence of inflation cannot be promised in advance.
