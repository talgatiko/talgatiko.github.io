# ИБ-004. A token in a repository: who failed to do their job?

[Все статьи / All articles](https://talgatiko.github.io/en/security/) · [Русский](https://talgatiko.github.io/ru/security/ib-004/) · [English](https://talgatiko.github.io/en/security/ib-004/)

This article continues the [manifesto against BDSM-InfoSec](https://talgatiko.github.io/en/security/ib-001/).

A developer receives a token, uses it in a program, and accidentally publishes it with the code in an open repository. The investigation begins: a violation, a written explanation, another lecture on information security.

Why did an ordinary developer have the keys to the apartment where the money is kept? Who gave them a token granting access to the customer database and company funds? Who decided it should remain valid for months?

The developer made a mistake. But the people who organized access had already determined what that mistake could cost.

## Millions of tokens — an accusation against whom?

We are shown articles about millions of tokens in public repositories. Look how irresponsible developers are. We need more bans, checks, and training. Pray to the church of InfoSec.

The number of published strings alone tells us nothing about how dangerous they are. A token may have expired long ago or work only in a training environment. If it still opens other people's data and money, the first questions belong with those responsible for protection: why was an ordinary employee mistake allowed to have such consequences?

To me, this is a question about the security team's own work. Its job is to limit the consequences of human mistakes in advance. If employees were issued dangerous tokens and then blamed for the failings of that arrangement, the team has not done its job properly.

That is what I call BDSM-InfoSec: turning a flaw in how work is organized into an employee's guilt, and presenting fear of punishment as protection.

## What needs to change

**Before:** the developer receives a token with broad access to the production system. Accidental publication could cost the company dearly.

**After — one option:** the developer receives a token only for their own isolated environment. Publishing it does not open the production system or real customer data. In both cases, the person receives a token. What changes is the access they have been given.

My employer has already put this approach into practice. A designated person is responsible for issuing tokens. Establishing this arrangement does not require building a dedicated automated system first. I propose making responsible access management a normal standard of work.

One option is to give the developer a token for a test environment or a personal isolated environment. They then have no need for real production secrets in everyday development.

Another option is to issue a token that works in production but has a strict time limit. Its lifetime is chosen with regard to the possible consequences of its use.

All tokens can be given a limited lifetime and replaced regularly. For a highly important system, change tokens and keys every hour if necessary. Exactly how replacement works is an implementation task.

## Whose responsibility is it?

The process should anticipate the accidental publication of an everyday work token. It should become neither a developer's sin nor an emergency. The problem lies in what access the person was given and how its use was organized.

If a published token is dangerous, replace it and deal with the consequences. Then fix the arrangement that allowed that string to become dangerous. Blaming the developer will not fix it.

A developer's job is to write programs. The job of those organizing protection is to arrange work so that a predictable human mistake does not put the company at risk. If your entire defense ends with “the developer has only themselves to blame,” that is BDSM-InfoSec.
