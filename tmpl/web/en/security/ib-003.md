# ИБ-003. Forgotten your password? Why is that my problem?

[Все статьи / All articles](https://talgatiko.github.io/en/security/) · [Русский](https://talgatiko.github.io/ru/security/ib-003/) · [English](https://talgatiko.github.io/en/security/ib-003/)

This article continues the [manifesto against BDSM-InfoSec](https://talgatiko.github.io/en/security/ib-001/).

A person returns to an online store. Enters a password. Rejected. Tries another. Rejected again. Clicks “Forgot password?”, opens an email, and invents a new one. Now they can finally do what they came to do.

Wait. If an email lets them replace the password, why can't it simply let them in? Why is a compulsory memory exercise standing between a person and a purchase?

## Who ordered this exam?

The service demanded a password. The service set the rules for composing it. The service decided that without this string, the person would get no work done. Yet when they forget it, the problem somehow becomes theirs: poor memory, improper storage, not taking security seriously enough.

Every site considers itself important enough to rent permanent space in someone else's memory. Naturally, it offers no rent. It does, however, send instructions on maintaining that space.

The person has to remember which password goes where, deal with rejected attempts, and once again prove they are entitled to open their own account. Meanwhile, the site already knows their email address and is willing to restore access through it. The service's owners organized this arrangement. The person did not choose it, but pays for it in time and frustration.

Inconvenience becomes a lesson in obedience: meet our demands first, then you may get on with your business. And if it doesn't work, you are to blame. That is the psychological side of BDSM-InfoSec.

## What I propose

I consider a one-time link through a personal communication channel a better sign-in method than a separate password. The person supplies an email address, phone number, or messenger account, receives a message, and confirms the sign-in. The link has a limited lifetime and no longer opens the account once used.

Most services do not need a separate mandatory password. Leave it to those who find it convenient. Give people a choice instead of an obligation to maintain another secret. The idea applies to any service; how often someone visits is beside the point.

If the user does not trust a single provider or wants stronger protection, sign-in should require confirmation through two channels. Both confirmations are required. Two apps with the same recovery route do not thereby become independent channels.

## What it could look like

The service sends an email or message containing a link. Instead of following it, the person could be offered a numeric code to enter on the sign-in page or, when needed, pass on verbally. The channel and convenient confirmation method can be chosen.

After sign-in, the service can remember the device instead of sending the person back to email every time they open the application. Otherwise, we merely replace “remember the password” with “find the email.” Changing payment details or granting important permissions can require separate confirmation that clearly describes the action.

## Whose job is it?

Email takeover remains a risk. But where that same email account is enough to reset a password, the risk existed already. Compare the proposal with that arrangement, not an imaginary system in which nobody makes mistakes. Existing additional protection must not be discarded.

Convenient, secure sign-in is the responsibility of the service owner and the people who organize it. They need to deliver the messages, plan for lost access to a channel, and avoid presenting a one-time link as protection against every kind of deception. The technical details are their job.

The user came to shop, write, or work. They did not sign up to become the keeper of your password collection.
